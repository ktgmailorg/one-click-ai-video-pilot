NARRATOR: White-looking light can contain many frequencies.

NARRATOR: A spectrometer separates those frequencies after light interacts with a

sample.

NARRATOR: Instead of asking only how bright the light is, spectroscopy asks which

frequencies are present, which are missing, and how strong each signal is.

NARRATOR: Atoms and molecules do not interact with every frequency equally.

NARRATOR: Their internal energy structure produces characteristic patterns of absorption

or emission.

NARRATOR: Those patterns act like spectral fingerprints.

NARRATOR: Matching the positions of measured peaks or missing bands with trusted reference

data can identify substances in an unknown sample.

NARRATOR: Peak position can indicate identity, while calibrated signal strength can help

estimate concentration.

NARRATOR: Conditions still matter: temperature, excitation, instrument response,

and overlapping signals can change what is observed.

NARRATOR: Spectroscopy is powerful because it connects a precise measurement to reference

physics, not because every colored line explains itself.
