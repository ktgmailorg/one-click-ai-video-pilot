NARRATOR: Phishing is not defined by bad spelling or email alone.

NARRATOR: It is a social-engineering attack that uses a message to trigger an action

useful to an attacker.

NARRATOR: That action might reveal a password, approve a sign-in, open malware, change

payment details, or disclose sensitive information.

NARRATOR: Messages can arrive through email, text, social platforms, collaboration tools,

phone calls, or QR codes.

NARRATOR: The attacker's advantage is context: deadlines, authority, fear, curiosity, and

routine business processes.

NARRATOR: This lesson builds a repeatable defense workflow: recognize suspicious

signals, inspect destinations safely, verify through an independent channel, report

quickly, protect accounts, and respond correctly if an action already occurred.

NARRATOR: The goal is not perfect intuition.

NARRATOR: It is a dependable process under pressure.

NARRATOR: Warning signs become useful when treated as reasons to pause, not as proof by

themselves.

NARRATOR: The message creates unusual urgency, asks for secrecy, invokes a senior leader,

promises an unexpected benefit, threatens an immediate consequence, or changes a normal

payment process.

NARRATOR: It may request a password, multifactor code, gift card, bank change,

document access, or software installation.

NARRATOR: A familiar logo or accurate personal detail does not make the message safe;

attackers can copy branding and use public information.

NARRATOR: Likewise, a legitimate message can contain a typo.

NARRATOR: Evaluate the requested action, context, and verification path together.

NARRATOR: The higher the consequence, the stronger the verification should be.

NARRATOR: A display name is only a label.

NARRATOR: Expand the sender information and inspect the complete address, including the

domain after the at sign.

NARRATOR: Look for look-alike spelling, unexpected consumer email services, or a

reply-to address that differs from the sender.

NARRATOR: For links, the visible text may not be the destination.

NARRATOR: On a desktop, preview the destination without opening it; on a phone,

use the platform's safe link-preview behavior if available.

NARRATOR: Read the registered domain from right to left before the first slash.

NARRATOR: Attackers can place a trusted name in a subdomain or path while the actual domain

belongs to them.

NARRATOR: Do not paste a suspicious link into a browser merely to see what happens.

NARRATOR: Independent verification breaks the attacker's control of the conversation.

NARRATOR: If a message asks for a payment change, call the requester using a number

already in your records.

NARRATOR: If it claims to be an account alert, open the official application or type the

organization's known address instead of following the embedded link.

NARRATOR: If a colleague requests sensitive data, contact them through a separate

established channel.

NARRATOR: Do not rely on a phone number, reply address, or support link included in the

suspicious message, because those can route back to the attacker.

NARRATOR: Verification should confirm both identity and requested action.

NARRATOR: Asking, 'Did you send this?' is weaker than confirming the specific file,

amount, destination, and business purpose.

NARRATOR: Links can lead to credential-harvesting sites or exploit

attempts.

NARRATOR: Attachments can contain malicious code, active content, or deceptive forms.

NARRATOR: QR codes hide their destination from normal visual inspection and can move the

interaction onto a less protected phone.

NARRATOR: The safe response is not to experiment.

NARRATOR: Report the item through your organization's approved mechanism and let

security tools or staff analyze it.

NARRATOR: If the document is genuinely needed, ask the sender to provide it through the

normal approved repository after verification.

NARRATOR: File extensions and icons can be misleading, and cloud-hosted files are not

automatically safe.

NARRATOR: Treat an unexpected delivery method as part of the risk signal.

NARRATOR: Credentials should be entered only into a known, verified sign-in flow.

NARRATOR: A message asking you to reply with a password or multifactor code is unsafe.

NARRATOR: A realistic fake sign-in page may capture the password and immediately ask for

the code, allowing an attacker to use both.

NARRATOR: Unexpected push notifications are also a warning.

NARRATOR: Deny the request rather than approving it to make the notifications stop,

then change the password from a trusted device and report the event.

NARRATOR: Password managers add a useful signal because they normally fill credentials

only on the exact saved domain.

NARRATOR: Phishing-resistant authentication methods further reduce risk, but no single

control replaces verification and reporting.

NARRATOR: Deleting a suspicious message protects only one inbox and may discard useful

evidence.

NARRATOR: Use the organization's phishing-report button, service desk, or

documented security channel before deletion.

NARRATOR: The original message contains headers, routing information, URLs, and

attachment identifiers that a screenshot or manual forward may omit.

NARRATOR: Reporting allows defenders to search for related messages, block infrastructure,

warn other recipients, and determine whether anyone interacted.

NARRATOR: Do not broadly forward the suspicious content to colleagues as a warning;

that can spread active links and attachments.

NARRATOR: Follow the approved process, add concise context about what looked suspicious

and whether you clicked, then delete or quarantine the item as instructed.

NARRATOR: If you clicked, entered credentials, approved a prompt, opened an attachment, or

sent information, stop and report immediately.

NARRATOR: Speed matters because defenders may be able to revoke sessions, reset credentials,

isolate a device, block transactions, or remove malicious messages before the impact

grows.

NARRATOR: Use a trusted device and official channel.

NARRATOR: Describe exactly what happened, including the time and information disclosed.

NARRATOR: Change affected passwords through the legitimate site and do not reuse the

replacement elsewhere.

NARRATOR: Follow organizational instructions before powering off or altering a potentially

compromised device, because evidence may matter.

NARRATOR: A fast honest report is a security control.

NARRATOR: Delayed disclosure gives the attacker more time.

NARRATOR: Organizations should not place the full burden on individual recipients.

NARRATOR: Secure email gateways, domain-authentication controls, attachment

analysis, URL protection, phishing-resistant multifactor authentication, least privilege,

logging, and rapid incident response reduce both likelihood and impact.

NARRATOR: Training should use the organization's real reporting workflow and

should measure constructive behavior, especially timely reporting, rather than

humiliating people who make mistakes.

NARRATOR: Finance and account-change processes need independent confirmation steps that

cannot be bypassed by one urgent message.

NARRATOR: Security teams need a way to remove malicious mail at scale and communicate

clearly during an incident.

NARRATOR: Human judgment works best as one layer in a system designed to expect

deception.

NARRATOR: Use one checklist whenever a message requests a consequential action.

NARRATOR: Pause before interacting.

NARRATOR: Inspect the full sender identity and destination.

NARRATOR: Ask whether the request, timing, tone, and delivery method fit the normal

process.

NARRATOR: Verify identity and action through a separately obtained channel.

NARRATOR: Report the original message using the approved mechanism, then remove it as

directed.

NARRATOR: If you already interacted, report immediately and follow containment steps.

NARRATOR: None of these checks depends on catching one spelling error or having perfect

technical knowledge.

NARRATOR: They create time and independent evidence—the two things a social-engineering

attack tries to remove.

NARRATOR: A secure response is not 'I knew it looked fake.'

NARRATOR: It is 'I followed a process that would still work

when the message looked real.'
