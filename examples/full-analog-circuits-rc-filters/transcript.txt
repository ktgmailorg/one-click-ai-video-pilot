NARRATOR: We begin by adopting the lumped circuit abstraction, which treats components

as localized entities where internal electromagnetic wave propagation is negligible

compared to signal travel times.

NARRATOR: This assumption allows us to model circuits as interconnected nodes and branches

rather than distributed fields.

NARRATOR: Imagine a simple battery connected to a resistor through short wires; the voltage

appears instantaneously across the resistor because the physical dimensions are tiny

relative to the signal wavelength.

NARRATOR: This abstraction holds when the circuit size is much smaller than the signal's

wavelength, ensuring that voltage and current are well-defined at each node.

NARRATOR: We will consistently use this model throughout our analysis.

NARRATOR: Next, we establish consistent reference polarities and current directions

for every element.

NARRATOR: This systematic approach prevents sign errors and ensures our equations reflect

physical reality.

NARRATOR: For example, if we assign the positive terminal of a battery to the top node

and define current flowing clockwise through the resistor, we must maintain these

conventions throughout the analysis.

NARRATOR: The direction we choose is arbitrary, but consistency is critical.

NARRATOR: When we later write equations, a positive voltage drop across a resistor means

current flows from the higher to lower potential, while a negative value indicates

the actual direction opposes our assumption.

NARRATOR: This disciplined labeling simplifies solving the resulting algebraic system.

NARRATOR: With reference directions set, we apply Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws to

constrain the circuit.

NARRATOR: Kirchhoff's current law states that the sum of currents entering any node equals

zero, reflecting charge conservation.

NARRATOR: Kirchhoff's voltage law requires that the sum of voltage drops around any

closed loop equals zero, reflecting energy conservation.

NARRATOR: For our series circuit, we write one KCL equation at the top node and one KVL

equation around the loop.

NARRATOR: These constraints reduce the number of unknowns and provide the structural

framework for solving the circuit.

NARRATOR: We then combine these with element laws to form a complete set of equations that

uniquely determine all voltages and currents.

NARRATOR: We now introduce the constitutive relations that describe how resistors and

capacitors behave.

NARRATOR: A resistor obeys Ohm's law, where voltage drop equals resistance times current.

NARRATOR: A capacitor relates current to the rate of voltage change, storing electric-field

energy as charge accumulates on its plates.

NARRATOR: These relations are fundamental because they connect electrical quantities to

physical mechanisms.

NARRATOR: For example, when current flows into a capacitor, voltage rises proportionally to

the accumulated charge.

NARRATOR: We use these laws alongside Kirchhoff constraints to build the complete

mathematical model of the circuit, ensuring every element's behavior is accurately

represented.

NARRATOR: When we combine a resistor and capacitor in series, the circuit exhibits a

characteristic time constant, tau, defined as resistance times capacitance.

NARRATOR: This value determines how quickly the circuit responds to changes.

NARRATOR: For example, if tau equals one second, the capacitor voltage will reach

approximately 63 percent of its final value after one second.

NARRATOR: The time constant emerges naturally from solving the differential equation that

governs the circuit.

NARRATOR: It represents the intrinsic speed of the system, balancing the resistor's

opposition to current flow with the capacitor's ability to store charge.

NARRATOR: Understanding tau allows us to predict response times without solving the

full equation every time.

NARRATOR: We now analyze how the capacitor charges and discharges when connected to a

voltage source.

NARRATOR: During charging, current flows from the source through the resistor into the

capacitor, causing voltage to rise exponentially.

NARRATOR: The differential equation governing this process yields a solution where voltage

approaches the source value asymptotically.

NARRATOR: Discharging occurs when the source is removed and the capacitor releases its

stored energy through the resistor.

NARRATOR: The voltage decays exponentially, following the same time constant but in

reverse.

NARRATOR: These behaviors demonstrate how energy transfers between the electric field

and the resistor, with the resistor dissipating energy as heat while the capacitor

temporarily stores it.

NARRATOR: We distinguish between transient and steady-state behavior to understand long-term

circuit operation.

NARRATOR: The transient phase occurs immediately after a change, characterized by

exponential charging or discharging governed by the time constant.

NARRATOR: The steady state emerges after several time constants, when the capacitor

voltage stabilizes at the source value.

NARRATOR: Initial capacitor voltage is continuous unless an impulse supplies

unbounded current, ensuring no sudden jumps in stored energy.

NARRATOR: Final steady-state behavior can be checked independently by replacing the

capacitor with a short circuit, which simplifies the analysis.

NARRATOR: This separation allows us to verify solutions and predict long-term performance

without solving the full differential equation.

NARRATOR: We now examine how the RC circuit functions as a low-pass filter, attenuating

high-frequency signals while passing low-frequency ones.

NARRATOR: When the input voltage changes slowly, the capacitor has time to charge

fully, allowing the output to follow the input closely.

NARRATOR: When the input changes rapidly, the capacitor cannot charge quickly enough, so the

output voltage lags and attenuates.

NARRATOR: This frequency-dependent behavior arises because the capacitor's impedance

decreases with increasing frequency.

NARRATOR: The circuit thus acts as a smooth filter, removing rapid fluctuations while

preserving slow variations.

NARRATOR: This property is essential in audio and signal processing applications where

clean, stable signals are required.

NARRATOR: We define the cutoff frequency, omega_c, as the point where the output voltage

drops to 35 percent of its input value.

NARRATOR: This frequency is inversely proportional to the time constant, calculated

as one divided by tau.

NARRATOR: Signals with frequencies below omega_c pass through with minimal attenuation,

while signals above omega_c are significantly reduced.

NARRATOR: The cutoff frequency provides a clear boundary for the circuit's frequency

response, indicating the highest frequency it can effectively transmit.

NARRATOR: By adjusting the resistor or capacitor values, we can shift this boundary

to filter different signal ranges.

NARRATOR: This parameter is crucial for designing filters that meet specific frequency

requirements.

NARRATOR: Finally, we calculate the complete step response and verify energy conservation.

NARRATOR: When a voltage step is applied, the capacitor charges exponentially, and the

resistor dissipates energy as heat.

NARRATOR: The total energy stored in the capacitor equals the energy supplied by the

source minus the energy dissipated in the resistor.

NARRATOR: Limiting cases at zero time, long time, zero frequency, and high frequency

provide useful error checks.

NARRATOR: Time-domain step response and frequency-domain filtering are complementary

descriptions of the same linear circuit behavior.

NARRATOR: By solving the differential equation and verifying these checks, we ensure our

solution is accurate and physically meaningful.
