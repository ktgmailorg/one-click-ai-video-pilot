We begin by adopting the lumped circuit abstraction, which treats components as

localized entities where internal electromagnetic wave propagation is negligible

compared to signal travel times.

This assumption allows us to model circuits as interconnected nodes and branches rather than

distributed fields.

Imagine a simple battery connected to a resistor through short wires; the voltage

appears instantaneously across the resistor because the physical dimensions are tiny

relative to the signal wavelength.

This abstraction holds when the circuit size is much smaller than the signal's wavelength,

ensuring that voltage and current are well-defined at each node.

We will consistently use this model throughout our analysis.

Next, we establish consistent reference polarities and current directions for every

element.

This systematic approach prevents sign errors and ensures our equations reflect physical

reality.

For example, if we assign the positive terminal of a battery to the top node and

define current flowing clockwise through the resistor, we must maintain these conventions

throughout the analysis.

The direction we choose is arbitrary, but consistency is critical.

When we later write equations, a positive voltage drop across a resistor means current

flows from the higher to lower potential, while a negative value indicates the actual

direction opposes our assumption.

This disciplined labeling simplifies solving the resulting algebraic system.

With reference directions set, we apply Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws to

constrain the circuit.

Kirchhoff's current law states that the sum of currents entering any node equals zero,

reflecting charge conservation.

Kirchhoff's voltage law requires that the sum of voltage drops around any closed loop equals

zero, reflecting energy conservation.

For our series circuit, we write one KCL equation at the top node and one KVL equation

around the loop.

These constraints reduce the number of unknowns and provide the structural framework

for solving the circuit.

We then combine these with element laws to form a complete set of equations that uniquely

determine all voltages and currents.

We now introduce the constitutive relations that describe how resistors and capacitors

behave.

A resistor obeys Ohm's law, where voltage drop equals resistance times current.

A capacitor relates current to the rate of voltage change, storing electric-field energy

as charge accumulates on its plates.

These relations are fundamental because they connect electrical quantities to physical

mechanisms.

For example, when current flows into a capacitor, voltage rises proportionally to the

accumulated charge.

We use these laws alongside Kirchhoff constraints to build the complete mathematical

model of the circuit, ensuring every element's behavior is accurately represented.

When we combine a resistor and capacitor in series, the circuit exhibits a characteristic

time constant, tau, defined as resistance times capacitance.

This value determines how quickly the circuit responds to changes.

For example, if tau equals one second, the capacitor voltage will reach approximately 63

percent of its final value after one second.

The time constant emerges naturally from solving the differential equation that governs

the circuit.

It represents the intrinsic speed of the system, balancing the resistor's opposition to

current flow with the capacitor's ability to store charge.

Understanding tau allows us to predict response times without solving the full

equation every time.

We now analyze how the capacitor charges and discharges when connected to a voltage source.

During charging, current flows from the source through the resistor into the capacitor,

causing voltage to rise exponentially.

The differential equation governing this process yields a solution where voltage

approaches the source value asymptotically.

Discharging occurs when the source is removed and the capacitor releases its stored energy

through the resistor.

The voltage decays exponentially, following the same time constant but in reverse.

These behaviors demonstrate how energy transfers between the electric field and the

resistor, with the resistor dissipating energy as heat while the capacitor temporarily stores

it.

We distinguish between transient and steady-state behavior to understand long-term

circuit operation.

The transient phase occurs immediately after a change, characterized by exponential charging

or discharging governed by the time constant.

The steady state emerges after several time constants, when the capacitor voltage

stabilizes at the source value.

Initial capacitor voltage is continuous unless an impulse supplies unbounded current,

ensuring no sudden jumps in stored energy.

Final steady-state behavior can be checked independently by replacing the capacitor with

a short circuit, which simplifies the analysis.

This separation allows us to verify solutions and predict long-term performance without

solving the full differential equation.

We now examine how the RC circuit functions as a low-pass filter, attenuating high-frequency

signals while passing low-frequency ones.

When the input voltage changes slowly, the capacitor has time to charge fully, allowing

the output to follow the input closely.

When the input changes rapidly, the capacitor cannot charge quickly enough, so the output

voltage lags and attenuates.

This frequency-dependent behavior arises because the capacitor's impedance decreases

with increasing frequency.

The circuit thus acts as a smooth filter, removing rapid fluctuations while preserving

slow variations.

This property is essential in audio and signal processing applications where clean, stable

signals are required.

We define the cutoff frequency, omega_c, as the point where the output voltage drops to 35

percent of its input value.

This frequency is inversely proportional to the time constant, calculated as one divided

by tau.

Signals with frequencies below omega_c pass through with minimal attenuation, while

signals above omega_c are significantly reduced.

The cutoff frequency provides a clear boundary for the circuit's frequency response,

indicating the highest frequency it can effectively transmit.

By adjusting the resistor or capacitor values, we can shift this boundary to filter different

signal ranges.

This parameter is crucial for designing filters that meet specific frequency

requirements.

Finally, we calculate the complete step response and verify energy conservation.

When a voltage step is applied, the capacitor charges exponentially, and the resistor

dissipates energy as heat.

The total energy stored in the capacitor equals the energy supplied by the source minus

the energy dissipated in the resistor.

Limiting cases at zero time, long time, zero frequency, and high frequency provide useful

error checks.

Time-domain step response and frequency-domain filtering are complementary descriptions of

the same linear circuit behavior.

By solving the differential equation and verifying these checks, we ensure our solution

is accurate and physically meaningful.
