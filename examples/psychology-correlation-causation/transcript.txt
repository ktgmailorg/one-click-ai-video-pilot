NARRATOR: When two measured variables change together, their data can show a correlation.

NARRATOR: A positive relationship means they tend to rise together; a negative relationship

means one tends to fall as the other rises.

NARRATOR: Correlation describes a pattern.

NARRATOR: It does not yet explain why the pattern exists.

NARRATOR: Ice-cream sales and reported crime can both rise during hot weather.

NARRATOR: That does not mean buying ice cream causes crime.

NARRATOR: Temperature changes behavior in ways that can affect both measurements.

NARRATOR: This third-variable problem is one reason an observed relationship cannot

establish a causal direction on its own.

NARRATOR: To support a causal claim, researchers need a design that manipulates an

independent variable and controls competing explanations, often with comparison groups and

random assignment.

NARRATOR: Observational correlations remain valuable for prediction and discovery.

NARRATOR: The professional habit is to make a claim no stronger than the study design

allows.
