NARRATOR: A controller is useful when a system must reach and maintain a target despite

disturbances and uncertainty.

NARRATOR: Imagine a motor commanded to run at one thousand revolutions per minute.

NARRATOR: An open-loop command may work under one load and miss the target under another.

NARRATOR: Feedback changes the structure of the problem.

NARRATOR: A sensor measures actual speed, the controller compares it with the desired speed,

and the difference becomes an error signal.

NARRATOR: The controller uses that error to change the motor input.

NARRATOR: The new speed is measured again, so correction is continuous rather than a

one-time guess.

NARRATOR: This lesson develops the proportional, integral, and derivative terms,

then connects their behavior to tuning, noise, saturation, and a practical design workflow.

NARRATOR: Start by naming every signal.

NARRATOR: The reference r of t is the desired behavior.

NARRATOR: The measured output y of t is what the sensor reports.

NARRATOR: Their difference, e of t, equals reference minus measured output.

NARRATOR: The controller converts error into a command u of t.

NARRATOR: An actuator applies that command to the plant, which is the physical or simulated

system being controlled.

NARRATOR: Disturbances can enter the plant, while sensor noise can enter the measurement

path.

NARRATOR: This vocabulary prevents a common mistake: the controller does not directly

control the error.

NARRATOR: It changes the plant input, observes the resulting output, and thereby influences

future error.

NARRATOR: Sign conventions also matter.

NARRATOR: With negative feedback, a positive error should normally produce a correction

that increases the output toward the reference.

NARRATOR: The proportional term responds to the error that exists right now.

NARRATOR: Its contribution is K p times e of t.

NARRATOR: If the error doubles, the proportional correction doubles.

NARRATOR: Increasing K p usually makes the response more forceful and can reduce rise

time, but excessive gain can produce overshoot, oscillation, or instability.

NARRATOR: Proportional action alone may also leave a steady offset.

NARRATOR: To hold a load against friction, for example, the actuator may require a nonzero

command.

NARRATOR: If command is proportional to error, a persistent error may be needed to produce

that command.

NARRATOR: This is not a defect in the formula; it is a consequence of the loop and plant.

NARRATOR: The important skill is to connect gain to observed response rather than

memorizing that larger is always better.

NARRATOR: The integral term responds to the history of error.

NARRATOR: Its state grows while error has one sign and shrinks when the sign reverses.

NARRATOR: A small error that persists can therefore produce a large enough correction to

eliminate the offset left by proportional control.

NARRATOR: This makes integral action powerful, but it also introduces memory.

NARRATOR: If the actuator saturates at its maximum while the error remains large, the

integral state can continue accumulating even though the plant cannot respond further.

NARRATOR: When the system finally approaches the target, that stored integral action may

drive it far past the reference.

NARRATOR: This is called integral windup.

NARRATOR: Practical controllers limit, pause, or back-calculate the integral state during

saturation.

NARRATOR: Integral action solves one problem while creating a responsibility to manage its

stored state.

NARRATOR: The derivative term reacts to how quickly the error is changing.

NARRATOR: If the output is racing toward the target, the error may still be positive but

decreasing rapidly.

NARRATOR: A derivative contribution can oppose that motion before the target is crossed,

adding damping and reducing overshoot.

NARRATOR: The difficulty is that differentiation amplifies rapid measurement

changes, including sensor noise.

NARRATOR: Real implementations therefore use a filtered derivative rather than an ideal

mathematical derivative.

NARRATOR: Many controllers also differentiate the measurement instead of the error, which

avoids a large derivative kick when the reference changes suddenly.

NARRATOR: Derivative action is not mandatory.

NARRATOR: A well-tuned PI controller is often sufficient, especially when sensors are noisy

or the plant already has adequate damping.

NARRATOR: A PID controller adds the three contributions into one command.

NARRATOR: Proportional action handles present error, integral action handles accumulated

error, and derivative action responds to trend.

NARRATOR: These descriptions are useful, but the terms interact through the same plant.

NARRATOR: Increasing K p changes the response that the integral and derivative terms

observe.

NARRATOR: Increasing K i may remove offset while reducing stability margin.

NARRATOR: Increasing K d may add damping while increasing noise sensitivity.

NARRATOR: The final command is also bounded by actuator voltage, force, valve opening, or

another physical limit.

NARRATOR: A controller equation without limits, sample time, sensor characteristics,

and plant dynamics is incomplete.

NARRATOR: Good tuning treats the loop as one system rather than adjusting three independent

knobs in isolation.

NARRATOR: A step response turns vague words like fast or stable into measurable criteria.

NARRATOR: Rise time describes how quickly output moves toward the new target.

NARRATOR: Peak overshoot measures how far it exceeds the target.

NARRATOR: Settling time measures how long it takes to remain inside an acceptable band.

NARRATOR: Steady-state error is the remaining difference after transients decay.

NARRATOR: These measures can conflict.

NARRATOR: A gain change that shortens rise time may increase overshoot.

NARRATOR: Integral action that removes final error may lengthen settling.

NARRATOR: Stability is the first requirement: a fast response that grows without bound is

not useful.

NARRATOR: Before tuning, define which measures matter for the application and what

constraints cannot be violated.

NARRATOR: A camera gimbal, temperature chamber, and chemical flow controller should

not share one generic tuning objective.

NARRATOR: A disciplined tuning process begins with safety and requirements.

NARRATOR: Confirm sensor polarity, actuator limits, and a safe test range.

NARRATOR: Start with integral and derivative action disabled.

NARRATOR: Increase proportional gain until the system responds clearly without unacceptable

oscillation.

NARRATOR: Add integral action gradually to remove persistent error, while watching

overshoot and windup.

NARRATOR: Add derivative action only when extra damping is needed and the measurement is

clean enough to support it.

NARRATOR: Then test reference changes, disturbances, load variation, saturation, and

noise.

NARRATOR: This sequence is not a universal proof of optimality, but it makes cause and

effect observable.

NARRATOR: More formal model-based methods can calculate gains, yet every real implementation

still requires validation against unmodeled behavior and physical limits.

NARRATOR: Most PID controllers run in software, so the continuous equation becomes a

sampled algorithm.

NARRATOR: The sample period must be short enough to observe relevant plant dynamics but

not so short that noise dominates.

NARRATOR: The integral becomes a running numerical sum.

NARRATOR: The derivative becomes a filtered difference between samples.

NARRATOR: Output clamping enforces actuator limits, and anti-windup keeps the integral

state consistent with those limits.

NARRATOR: Systems that switch between manual and automatic control also need bumpless

transfer so the stored controller state does not create a sudden command jump.

NARRATOR: Record the sample time and exact algorithm with the gains; K p, K i, and K d

have meaning only within a defined implementation and unit convention.

NARRATOR: A reliable PID analysis answers six questions.

NARRATOR: What are the reference, measured output, error, command, plant, and

disturbances?

NARRATOR: What behavior does proportional, integral, and derivative action contribute in

this loop?

NARRATOR: Which performance measures define success?

NARRATOR: Where can the actuator or sensor saturate?

NARRATOR: How are sample time, filtering, and anti-windup implemented?

NARRATOR: And has the design been tested across the real operating range?

NARRATOR: The goal is not to use all three letters.

NARRATOR: The goal is a stable, understandable controller that meets requirements with

appropriate complexity.

NARRATOR: Sometimes that is P, often PI, and sometimes full PID.

NARRATOR: If you can explain the loop and predict the consequence of each change before

turning a gain, you are tuning as an engineer rather than searching blindly.
