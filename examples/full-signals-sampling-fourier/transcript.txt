NARRATOR: We begin by distinguishing how signals exist in time.

NARRATOR: A continuous signal varies smoothly without interruption, like the voltage across

a capacitor charging gradually.

NARRATOR: Mathematically, we describe it with a function that accepts any real number for

time.

NARRATOR: A discrete signal, however, only exists at specific, separate moments.

NARRATOR: Think of a metronome ticking or a digital audio file recording sound only at

fixed intervals.

NARRATOR: The key difference lies in the domain: continuous signals use real-valued

time, while discrete signals use integer indices.

NARRATOR: This distinction shapes how we analyze, store, and process information in

engineering systems.

NARRATOR: Understanding this boundary is essential before we model how systems respond

to these different input types.

NARRATOR: Next, we model how systems process these signals.

NARRATOR: A linear time-invariant system behaves consistently regardless of when you

apply an input.

NARRATOR: Linearity means doubling the input doubles the output, and time-invariance means

the system's behavior doesn't shift over time.

NARRATOR: We characterize such systems using differential equations for continuous domains

or difference equations for discrete domains.

NARRATOR: These equations capture how the system's internal state evolves.

NARRATOR: By solving them, we predict output behavior without simulating every possible

input.

NARRATOR: This mathematical framework allows us to analyze complex circuits or

communication channels using fundamental building blocks rather than trial-and-error

experimentation.

NARRATOR: To fully understand an LTI system, we examine its response to a single,

instantaneous spike called an impulse.

NARRATOR: This impulse response reveals exactly how the system reacts to any arbitrary

input.

NARRATOR: When we feed a general signal into the system, the output emerges from combining

the impulse response with the input through convolution.

NARRATOR: Convolution mathematically slides the impulse response across the input, scales

it, and sums the products at each time point.

NARRATOR: This operation captures how past inputs influence the current output.

NARRATOR: By mastering convolution, we can predict system behavior for complex waveforms

without solving differential equations from scratch, making it a cornerstone of signal

processing analysis.

NARRATOR: To analyze signals across frequencies, we use complex exponentials.

NARRATOR: These functions oscillate with constant magnitude while rotating in the

complex plane, making them ideal for representing sinusoidal waves.

NARRATOR: The Fourier representation decomposes any signal into a sum of these

complex exponentials, revealing its frequency content.

NARRATOR: Instead of working with time-domain waveforms, we shift to the frequency domain

where signals become vectors of complex coefficients.

NARRATOR: This transformation simplifies system analysis because multiplication in the

frequency domain corresponds to convolution in the time domain.

NARRATOR: Engineers use this view to design filters and modulators by manipulating

frequency components directly rather than solving differential equations.

NARRATOR: The frequency response describes how an LTI system modifies different frequency

components.

NARRATOR: It shows both magnitude attenuation and phase shift across the spectrum.

NARRATOR: When a signal passes through a system, each complex exponential component

gets scaled and rotated according to the system's frequency response at that specific

frequency.

NARRATOR: This means filters can selectively amplify or suppress certain frequencies while

preserving others.

NARRATOR: By examining the magnitude plot, we see which frequencies pass through unchanged,

and the phase plot reveals timing delays.

NARRATOR: Consistent predictions between time and frequency domains confirm our model

accurately captures the system's behavior, allowing reliable design of audio equalizers

or communication channels.

NARRATOR: When we convert a continuous signal to digital form, we sample it at regular

intervals.

NARRATOR: This process creates repeated copies of the original spectrum, spaced by the

sampling frequency.

NARRATOR: Each copy represents the same frequency content but shifted in the frequency

domain.

NARRATOR: If the original signal contains frequencies higher than half the sampling

rate, these shifted copies will overlap with the base spectrum.

NARRATOR: This overlap makes distinct frequencies indistinguishable after sampling,

a phenomenon called aliasing.

NARRATOR: To prevent this, we must ensure the signal is bandlimited and apply anti-aliasing

filters before sampling.

NARRATOR: Proper sampling preserves the original frequency information without

distortion.

NARRATOR: Reconstructing a continuous signal from discrete samples requires careful

workflow planning.

NARRATOR: First, we identify the signal's maximum frequency to determine its bandwidth.

NARRATOR: Next, we apply an analog low-pass filter to remove frequencies above half the

sampling rate, preventing spectral overlap.

NARRATOR: We then select a sample rate with sufficient margin to avoid aliasing entirely.

NARRATOR: Finally, we verify that the reconstruction process accurately recovers the

original waveform.

NARRATOR: Increasing the sample rate after aliasing has occurred cannot repair the lost

information, as the overlapping frequencies are permanently mixed.

NARRATOR: This systematic approach ensures digital systems faithfully represent analog

signals for further processing or storage.

NARRATOR: Let's trace a single sinusoidal signal through a practical filter and sampler.

NARRATOR: The input is a pure tone at a specific frequency.

NARRATOR: The filter first shapes the spectrum, attenuating unwanted frequencies

while preserving the target tone.

NARRATOR: The sampler then captures this filtered signal at discrete time points.

NARRATOR: If the tone's frequency falls within the passband and below the Nyquist limit, the

sampled values will accurately represent the original wave.

NARRATOR: We can verify this by reconstructing the samples and comparing them to the input.

NARRATOR: This end-to-end trace demonstrates how filtering and sampling work together to

preserve frequency information while enabling digital processing.

NARRATOR: Engineers use this principle to design communication systems that transmit

audio or data without distortion.

NARRATOR: A fundamental principle in signals and systems is that equivalent time- and

frequency-domain views must produce consistent predictions.

NARRATOR: When we analyze a system using differential equations in the time domain, we

should get the same output as when we use convolution with the impulse response.

NARRATOR: Similarly, frequency-domain multiplication should match time-domain

results.

NARRATOR: This consistency allows engineers to switch between domains based on which is

mathematically simpler.

NARRATOR: For example, we might solve a complex circuit using Laplace transforms in

the frequency domain, then verify the results by simulating the time-domain response.

NARRATOR: This cross-validation ensures our models accurately capture physical behavior

and prevents design errors in real-world applications.

NARRATOR: To apply these concepts, we use a practical analysis checklist.

NARRATOR: First, identify whether the signal is continuous or discrete and determine its

bandwidth.

NARRATOR: Next, model the system as LTI and obtain its impulse response.

NARRATOR: Verify that the signal frequency falls within the passband and below the

Nyquist rate.

NARRATOR: Apply anti-aliasing filtering if necessary, then select a sample rate with

margin.

NARRATOR: Finally, trace the signal through the filter and sampler, checking that

reconstructed values match the original.

NARRATOR: This checklist ensures systematic design, prevents aliasing, and validates

frequency preservation.

NARRATOR: By following these steps, engineers can confidently build reliable signal

processing systems that meet performance requirements across diverse applications.
