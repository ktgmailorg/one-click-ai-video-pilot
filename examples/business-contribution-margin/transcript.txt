Imagine a product sells for one hundred dollars and requires sixty dollars of variable

cost for each unit.

The forty-dollar difference is its unit contribution margin.

It is not automatically profit.

It is the amount that one additional sale contributes toward fixed costs and, after

those are covered, operating profit.

Divide forty dollars by the one-hundred-dollar selling price and the contribution margin

ratio is forty percent.

That means forty cents of each sales dollar is available for fixed costs and then profit,

under the model's assumptions.

Ratios make it easier to compare products with different prices.

Managers use contribution margin to explore break-even volume, product mix, and the effect

of changing price or variable cost.

The result is only as good as the assumptions.

Costs may change outside a relevant range, capacity may be limited, and demand may

respond to price.

Contribution margin is a decision model, not a substitute for context.
