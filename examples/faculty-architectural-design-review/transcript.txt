A useful critique starts by restating what the project is trying to accomplish.

Imagine a small community learning center on a sloped urban site.

Its intent is to create an accessible public threshold, flexible classrooms, daylight, and

a visible connection to the neighborhood.

Before evaluating form, the reviewer names the users, program, site conditions, and

performance goals.

This gives every later comment a reason.

Without intent, critique collapses into personal preference; with intent, each

observation can test whether the proposal supports the project's stated aims.

No single drawing tells the full story.

The site plan explains approach and context.

The floor plan reveals circulation and adjacencies.

The section shows level changes, daylight, structure, and vertical relationships.

Elevations describe public face and environmental response, while details test

whether the envelope can actually perform.

A strong review traces one design question across all of them.

For example: can a visitor move from sidewalk to entrance, reception, and classroom without

ambiguity or barriers?

Contradictions between drawings become high-value revision targets.

Critique becomes actionable when comments are anchored to visible evidence.

Instead of saying, 'This plan is confusing,' point to the crossing circulation paths and

say, 'Visitors arriving here meet two equally prominent routes, so orientation is uncertain.

Which destination should be visually primary?

' The annotation records an observation, its consequence, and a design question.

It does not prescribe the only answer or evaluate the designer as a person.

This structure preserves authorship while making the review precise enough to revisit

during revision.

Architecture becomes credible when systems reinforce the spatial idea.

A roof that brings daylight may also carry structure, drainage, insulation, and

mechanical distribution.

Moving one edge can alter energy performance, acoustics, cost, and the experience of the

room.

An integrative review therefore asks what each change improves and what it affects elsewhere.

Layered overlays make dependencies visible.

The goal is not maximum complexity; it is coordination, so environmental and technical

performance strengthen rather than undermine the intended experience.

The review ends with a revision plan.

Group comments by issue, cite the drawing evidence, identify every affected view, assign

priority, and define the next test.

A high-priority access conflict might require plan and section changes plus a circulation

diagram.

A facade rhythm concern might be tested with two elevation alternatives.

The matrix does not turn design into a checklist; it protects the reasoning from

being lost after the conversation.

Students can revisit the recorded critique, locate each moment, and show how the next

iteration responds.
