An oral-history interview is not a transparent window into the past.

It is a historical source created in a particular encounter.

The narrator chooses what to remember and how to tell it; the interviewer shapes the path

through questions and reactions; the recording medium and archive shape what later audiences

can access.

Begin with the source itself: who participated, when and where it was recorded,

what language or languages were used, what the project asked about, and what permissions

govern use.

A disciplined reading separates three layers.

Testimony is what the narrator communicates.

Context includes institutions, events, places, and social relationships that make the account

legible.

Interpretation is the argument a researcher builds by relating the interview to other

evidence.

Keeping these layers visible prevents an editor from presenting an inference as the

narrator's own claim.

It also preserves disagreement.

Memory can be selective without being useless; the way an event is remembered may itself

reveal identity, community, or later change.

For Deaf oral histories, access is not a final technical add-on.

Language, interpretation, captioning, transcription, camera framing, and interface

design affect what can be expressed and what audiences receive.

A transcript can support search and quotation, but it does not replace the embodied and

linguistic information in a signed or spoken recording.

Editors should document who translated or interpreted, preserve the original recording,

and avoid forcing culturally specific expression into misleading written

equivalents.

Accessible presentation and historical accuracy are intertwined.

Corroboration does not mean treating the narrator like a suspect.

It means building a richer evidence field.

Compare dates and names with club minutes, newspapers, photographs, directories, policy

documents, and other interviews.

When sources conflict, record the difference and ask what each source was positioned to

know.

A club record may document an official decision; an interview may reveal how that

decision felt, who was excluded, or how community members adapted.

Neither source automatically cancels the other.

Before publishing, confirm that use matches the recorded permission and that names and

sensitive material are handled as agreed.

Provide accurate captions, a reviewed transcript, descriptive context, and

provenance that identifies the collection and editorial choices.

Do not simulate sign language or invent a narrator's voice.

Make it easy for audiences to locate the original source and for participants or

communities to raise corrections.

Ethical oral history preserves agency while enabling study; accessibility is part of that

preservation, not separate from it.
