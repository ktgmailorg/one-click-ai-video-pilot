NARRATOR: A community nutrition program is not defined by the information it distributes.

NARRATOR: It is defined by a nutrition need, a community context, a plausible change

strategy, and a decision process.

NARRATOR: Begin by asking who needs to decide what.

NARRATOR: A health department may need to choose which neighborhood to serve.

NARRATOR: A school partnership may need to revise delivery.

NARRATOR: A funder may need evidence of reach and benefit.

NARRATOR: That decision determines which community voices, data, activities, and

outcomes matter.

NARRATOR: In this lesson, we will move from assessment to a logic model, then from

measurement to action.

NARRATOR: The result is not a fictional perfect program.

NARRATOR: It is a bounded design that makes assumptions visible, treats community members

as partners, and can be improved when evidence contradicts the plan.

NARRATOR: "The community" must be operationally defined.

NARRATOR: It may be a geographic area, a school network, a cultural group, a service

population, or overlapping communities.

NARRATOR: State the boundary and who may be excluded by it.

NARRATOR: Then assemble a comprehensive picture: health outcomes, dietary patterns,

food access, economic and environmental conditions, existing services, community

assets, and experiences that explain the numbers.

NARRATOR: Use multiple sources because no single dataset represents the whole system.

NARRATOR: Administrative data can show service use.

NARRATOR: Surveys can estimate behaviors.

NARRATOR: Interviews and listening sessions can reveal barriers, trust, and priorities.

NARRATOR: Asset mapping shows what already works.

NARRATOR: Assessment is not a hunt for deficits.

NARRATOR: It is a shared interpretation of needs, strengths, resources, and constraints

that can support a legitimate priority.

NARRATOR: Engagement is not one feedback meeting after the program has been designed.

NARRATOR: Identify residents affected by the issue, organizations already doing related

work, implementers, funders, data stewards, and people who can remove barriers.

NARRATOR: Clarify which decisions each partner can influence.

NARRATOR: Compensate participation when possible and provide information in accessible

forms.

NARRATOR: Ask whose interpretation is missing, who may be burdened by data collection, and

whose trust is required for the work to continue.

NARRATOR: Then establish a transparent priority rule.

NARRATOR: A team might compare magnitude, disparity, urgency, feasibility, existing

assets, community concern, and potential benefit.

NARRATOR: The weights are value judgments, so they should be visible.

NARRATOR: Collaborative priority setting improves both relevance and the chances that a

program will be used.

NARRATOR: Turn a broad concern into a statement that can guide action.

NARRATOR: Name the population, nutrition-related condition or behavior,

place, time period, comparison, and evidence.

NARRATOR: Separate observation from explanation.

NARRATOR: "Fruit and vegetable intake is low" is incomplete without the instrument,

threshold, population, and uncertainty.

NARRATOR: "Access causes the behavior" is a hypothesis until supported.

NARRATOR: Add the community assets that could change the condition and the structural

constraints that a nutrition class alone cannot solve.

NARRATOR: Avoid defining people as the problem.

NARRATOR: The statement should locate a modifiable part of a system while recognizing

conditions outside the program's control.

NARRATOR: Finish with a decision question, such as whether a particular service strategy

can reach the intended group and improve a measured intermediate outcome within one

program cycle.

NARRATOR: A logic model makes the program's causal story reviewable.

NARRATOR: Inputs are resources such as staff, partner time, space, food, data, and funding.

NARRATOR: Activities are what the program does.

NARRATOR: Outputs are direct products: sessions delivered, people reached, referrals

completed, or environmental changes adopted.

NARRATOR: Outcomes are changes expected after those outputs.

NARRATOR: Short-term outcomes can include knowledge, confidence, goals, readiness, or

partner capacity.

NARRATOR: Medium-term outcomes can include behavior or setting changes.

NARRATOR: Long-term outcomes concern maintenance and population results.

NARRATOR: Draw arrows only when the team can state why one element should influence the

next.

NARRATOR: Beneath each arrow, record assumptions and external factors.

NARRATOR: A logic model is not proof.

NARRATOR: It is a compact set of hypotheses that tells the evaluator what evidence would

strengthen or weaken the program theory.

NARRATOR: Match the intervention to the cause the assessment identifies.

NARRATOR: A knowledge barrier may justify education, but education alone cannot change

store inventory, transportation, prices, scheduling, or organizational policy.

NARRATOR: Individual-level strategies can build skills and confidence.

NARRATOR: Setting-level strategies can change availability, placement, procurement, or

service routines.

NARRATOR: Partnership and sector strategies can coordinate resources or policies across

organizations.

NARRATOR: A multilevel program should explain how the parts reinforce one another.

NARRATOR: Define the dose, reach, delivery setting, implementer, adaptation rules, and

fidelity elements that must remain.

NARRATOR: Also name what the program does not claim to change.

NARRATOR: A well-bounded strategy directs resources toward a modifiable mechanism

instead of asking participants to overcome a structural barrier the program leaves

untouched.

NARRATOR: Choose measures from the evaluation question, not from convenience alone.

NARRATOR: A 24-hour recall, food record, food-frequency questionnaire, and brief

screener capture different time frames and impose different burdens.

NARRATOR: Self-report is affected by memory, reactivity, day-to-day variation, and

systematic reporting bias.

NARRATOR: Repeating a measure can reduce some random variability, but repetition does not

automatically remove systematic bias.

NARRATOR: Administrative records, direct observation, environmental audits, or

biomarkers may answer different questions and introduce different constraints.

NARRATOR: Use a validated instrument for the intended population when available, pilot the

process, and document missing data.

NARRATOR: State whether the outcome concerns an individual, a group mean, change over time,

reach, or implementation.

NARRATOR: Measurement is part of the program design because weak evidence can produce a

confident but wrong decision.

NARRATOR: An evaluation cannot answer every interesting question.

NARRATOR: Prioritize those that partners can use.

NARRATOR: Separate implementation questions—who was reached, what was delivered,

and with what fidelity—from outcome questions about change.

NARRATOR: Specify the indicator, data source, collection time, comparison, and analysis

before looking at the result.

NARRATOR: A pre-post change may be useful for program improvement but does not by itself

establish that the program caused the change.

NARRATOR: A comparison group can strengthen a causal claim, but only when selection, timing,

contamination, and ethics are addressed.

NARRATOR: Qualitative evidence can explain why reach or outcomes differ across groups.

NARRATOR: Document protocol changes and uncertainty.

NARRATOR: Credibility comes from a design proportionate to the decision, not from using

the most complicated method available.

NARRATOR: Consider a hypothetical neighborhood where partners identify cost, transportation,

and unfamiliar preparation as barriers to produce use.

NARRATOR: The assets include a trusted community center, a mobile market, and

resident educators.

NARRATOR: The pilot combines scheduled produce access with optional preparation

demonstrations and referral support.

NARRATOR: Outputs include delivery reliability, households reached, repeat

participation, and referrals completed.

NARRATOR: Short-term outcomes include confidence and intent.

NARRATOR: Medium-term outcomes may include reported purchasing or preparation behavior

and organizational adoption.

NARRATOR: The team also tracks price, missed stops, participant burden, and differences in

reach across subgroups.

NARRATOR: This example does not prove the strategy works.

NARRATOR: It shows how assessment evidence, partner roles, a logic model, measures, and

decision rules can be connected before launch.

NARRATOR: The final question is whether evidence supports expansion, revision, or

stopping.

NARRATOR: Complete the design with a decision record.

NARRATOR: What context and community boundary are in scope?

NARRATOR: Who shares authority?

NARRATOR: Which assessed priority was selected, and by what rule?

NARRATOR: What change mechanism does the logic model propose?

NARRATOR: Which activities and outputs operationalize it?

NARRATOR: What outcomes and measures match the decision?

NARRATOR: What evidence and comparison support the conclusion?

NARRATOR: How did reach, burden, and results differ across groups?

NARRATOR: Which limitations could change the interpretation?

NARRATOR: Finally, who will act, by when, and what result will trigger continuation,

revision, scale, or stop?

NARRATOR: Share findings in forms each audience can use, including negative or

uncertain results.

NARRATOR: Community nutrition improves when evaluation is not an after-action report, but

a transparent learning system embedded from assessment through the next decision.
