A community nutrition program is not defined by the information it distributes.

It is defined by a nutrition need, a community context, a plausible change strategy, and a

decision process.

Begin by asking who needs to decide what.

A health department may need to choose which neighborhood to serve.

A school partnership may need to revise delivery.

A funder may need evidence of reach and benefit.

That decision determines which community voices, data, activities, and outcomes matter.

In this lesson, we will move from assessment to a logic model, then from measurement to

action.

The result is not a fictional perfect program.

It is a bounded design that makes assumptions visible, treats community members as partners,

and can be improved when evidence contradicts the plan.

"The community" must be operationally defined.

It may be a geographic area, a school network, a cultural group, a service population, or

overlapping communities.

State the boundary and who may be excluded by it.

Then assemble a comprehensive picture: health outcomes, dietary patterns, food access,

economic and environmental conditions, existing services, community assets, and

experiences that explain the numbers.

Use multiple sources because no single dataset represents the whole system.

Administrative data can show service use.

Surveys can estimate behaviors.

Interviews and listening sessions can reveal barriers, trust, and priorities.

Asset mapping shows what already works.

Assessment is not a hunt for deficits.

It is a shared interpretation of needs, strengths, resources, and constraints that can

support a legitimate priority.

Engagement is not one feedback meeting after the program has been designed.

Identify residents affected by the issue, organizations already doing related work,

implementers, funders, data stewards, and people who can remove barriers.

Clarify which decisions each partner can influence.

Compensate participation when possible and provide information in accessible forms.

Ask whose interpretation is missing, who may be burdened by data collection, and whose

trust is required for the work to continue.

Then establish a transparent priority rule.

A team might compare magnitude, disparity, urgency, feasibility, existing assets,

community concern, and potential benefit.

The weights are value judgments, so they should be visible.

Collaborative priority setting improves both relevance and the chances that a program will

be used.

Turn a broad concern into a statement that can guide action.

Name the population, nutrition-related condition or behavior, place, time period,

comparison, and evidence.

Separate observation from explanation.

"Fruit and vegetable intake is low" is incomplete without the instrument, threshold,

population, and uncertainty.

"Access causes the behavior" is a hypothesis until supported.

Add the community assets that could change the condition and the structural constraints that

a nutrition class alone cannot solve.

Avoid defining people as the problem.

The statement should locate a modifiable part of a system while recognizing conditions

outside the program's control.

Finish with a decision question, such as whether a particular service strategy can

reach the intended group and improve a measured intermediate outcome within one

program cycle.

A logic model makes the program's causal story reviewable.

Inputs are resources such as staff, partner time, space, food, data, and funding.

Activities are what the program does.

Outputs are direct products: sessions delivered, people reached, referrals

completed, or environmental changes adopted.

Outcomes are changes expected after those outputs.

Short-term outcomes can include knowledge, confidence, goals, readiness, or partner

capacity.

Medium-term outcomes can include behavior or setting changes.

Long-term outcomes concern maintenance and population results.

Draw arrows only when the team can state why one element should influence the next.

Beneath each arrow, record assumptions and external factors.

A logic model is not proof.

It is a compact set of hypotheses that tells the evaluator what evidence would strengthen

or weaken the program theory.

Match the intervention to the cause the assessment identifies.

A knowledge barrier may justify education, but education alone cannot change store inventory,

transportation, prices, scheduling, or organizational policy.

Individual-level strategies can build skills and confidence.

Setting-level strategies can change availability, placement, procurement, or

service routines.

Partnership and sector strategies can coordinate resources or policies across

organizations.

A multilevel program should explain how the parts reinforce one another.

Define the dose, reach, delivery setting, implementer, adaptation rules, and fidelity

elements that must remain.

Also name what the program does not claim to change.

A well-bounded strategy directs resources toward a modifiable mechanism instead of

asking participants to overcome a structural barrier the program leaves untouched.

Choose measures from the evaluation question, not from convenience alone.

A 24-hour recall, food record, food-frequency questionnaire, and brief screener capture

different time frames and impose different burdens.

Self-report is affected by memory, reactivity, day-to-day variation, and systematic reporting

bias.

Repeating a measure can reduce some random variability, but repetition does not

automatically remove systematic bias.

Administrative records, direct observation, environmental audits, or biomarkers may answer

different questions and introduce different constraints.

Use a validated instrument for the intended population when available, pilot the process,

and document missing data.

State whether the outcome concerns an individual, a group mean, change over time,

reach, or implementation.

Measurement is part of the program design because weak evidence can produce a confident

but wrong decision.

An evaluation cannot answer every interesting question.

Prioritize those that partners can use.

Separate implementation questions—who was reached, what was delivered, and with what

fidelity—from outcome questions about change.

Specify the indicator, data source, collection time, comparison, and analysis before looking

at the result.

A pre-post change may be useful for program improvement but does not by itself establish

that the program caused the change.

A comparison group can strengthen a causal claim, but only when selection, timing,

contamination, and ethics are addressed.

Qualitative evidence can explain why reach or outcomes differ across groups.

Document protocol changes and uncertainty.

Credibility comes from a design proportionate to the decision, not from using the most

complicated method available.

Consider a hypothetical neighborhood where partners identify cost, transportation, and

unfamiliar preparation as barriers to produce use.

The assets include a trusted community center, a mobile market, and resident educators.

The pilot combines scheduled produce access with optional preparation demonstrations and

referral support.

Outputs include delivery reliability, households reached, repeat participation, and

referrals completed.

Short-term outcomes include confidence and intent.

Medium-term outcomes may include reported purchasing or preparation behavior and

organizational adoption.

The team also tracks price, missed stops, participant burden, and differences in reach

across subgroups.

This example does not prove the strategy works.

It shows how assessment evidence, partner roles, a logic model, measures, and decision

rules can be connected before launch.

The final question is whether evidence supports expansion, revision, or stopping.

Complete the design with a decision record.

What context and community boundary are in scope?

Who shares authority?

Which assessed priority was selected, and by what rule?

What change mechanism does the logic model propose?

Which activities and outputs operationalize it?

What outcomes and measures match the decision?

What evidence and comparison support the conclusion?

How did reach, burden, and results differ across groups?

Which limitations could change the interpretation?

Finally, who will act, by when, and what result will trigger continuation, revision,

scale, or stop?

Share findings in forms each audience can use, including negative or uncertain results.

Community nutrition improves when evaluation is not an after-action report, but a

transparent learning system embedded from assessment through the next decision.
