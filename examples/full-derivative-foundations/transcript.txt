This lesson builds the derivative from first principles.

We will begin with an average rate of change over an interval, shrink that interval using a

limit, and obtain the derivative at one point.

Then we will turn that pointwise idea into a derivative function, recognize where

differentiability fails, interpret motion, and use a tangent line for local approximation.

The goal is not merely to memorize f prime notation.

It is to understand what information a derivative contains, how the definition

produces it, and when that information is valid.

Keep one question in view: how can we describe change at an exact input when an ordinary

slope calculation needs two different points?

Choose an input a and a second input a plus h.

The change in input is h.

The corresponding change in output is f of a plus h minus f of a. Dividing output change by

input change gives the difference quotient.

Geometrically, it is the slope of the secant line through the two points.

In an application, it is an average rate across the interval.

If position changes by one hundred meters over five seconds, the average velocity is twenty

meters per second, even if the object moved faster or slower within those five seconds.

Average change summarizes an interval.

It does not yet isolate what happens at a single instant.

To focus on the input a, let h approach zero.

We do not substitute h equals zero into the quotient; that would divide by zero.

Instead, we examine values of h that are nonzero but arbitrarily small.

As the second point approaches the first, the secant lines may approach a limiting line.

If their slopes approach one finite value from both sides, that value is f prime of a, the

derivative at a.

The limit is the crucial reasoning step.

It converts a family of nearby average rates into one instantaneous rate.

If the slopes do not settle to a common finite value, the derivative at that point does not

exist.

Let f of x equal x squared.

Start with f of a plus h minus f of a, all divided by h. Expanding gives a squared plus

two a h plus h squared minus a squared, divided by h.

The a-squared terms cancel.

Because h is nonzero during the limiting process, factor h from the numerator and

cancel it with the denominator.

The quotient becomes two a plus h.

Now let h approach zero.

The limit is two a. Notice the order of operations: form the quotient, simplify it for

nonzero h, then evaluate the limit.

We never claim that h itself is zero while it remains in the denominator.

The previous calculation kept a symbolic, so it did more than find one slope.

It produced a rule: for f of x equals x squared, f prime of x equals two x.

This new function reports the original curve's tangent slope at every differentiable input.

At x equals three, the derivative is six.

At x equals negative two, it is negative four.

At zero, it is zero.

The derivative function has its own graph and domain.

Its input is still an x-value from the original problem, while its output is a rate

or slope.

Thinking of f prime as a function is essential for later topics such as optimization and

differential equations.

The sign of the derivative summarizes local direction.

Where f prime is positive, tangent lines rise from left to right, so the function is locally

increasing.

Where f prime is negative, tangent lines fall, so the function is locally decreasing.

Where f prime equals zero, the tangent is horizontal.

A zero derivative identifies a critical point candidate, not automatically a maximum or

minimum.

For x squared, f prime changes from negative to positive at zero, matching the parabola's

minimum.

But another function can have a horizontal tangent and continue increasing.

The derivative gives local evidence; interpreting a larger pattern requires

checking how that evidence changes around the point.

Not every graph has an ordinary finite tangent slope everywhere.

At a jump or hole, the function is not continuous, so it cannot be differentiable

there.

At a sharp corner, the left-hand and right-hand slopes approach different values.

At a cusp, the slopes can diverge with incompatible signs.

At a vertical tangent, the slope becomes unbounded rather than approaching a finite

number.

Differentiability is therefore a stronger condition than continuity.

If a function is differentiable at a point, it is continuous there.

The reverse is not guaranteed: the absolute-value function is continuous at zero

but has a corner, so its derivative at zero does not exist.

Suppose s of t gives position along a line.

A secant slope over a time interval gives average velocity.

The derivative s prime of t gives instantaneous velocity.

Its sign indicates direction relative to the chosen coordinate axis, while its magnitude is

speed.

Differentiate velocity to obtain acceleration.

Positive acceleration does not necessarily mean the object is speeding up; speed

increases when velocity and acceleration have the same sign and decreases when their signs

differ.

Units also reveal meaning.

If position is measured in meters and time in seconds, velocity uses meters per second and

acceleration uses meters per second squared.

The derivative is also the coefficient of a local linear model.

At x equals a, the tangent line has value f of a and slope f prime of a.

Its equation is L of x equals f of a plus f prime of a times x minus a.

For x close to a, L of x can approximate f of x.

For example, to estimate the square root of four point one, use f of x equals square root

x at a equals four.

The value is two and the derivative is one fourth, so the estimate is two plus one fourth

times zero point one, or two point zero two five.

The method works locally; accuracy generally decreases farther from the base point.

Let f of x equal three x squared minus two x, and find the tangent at x equals one.

The derivative function is six x minus two, so f prime of one is four.

The point on the original graph is one comma f of one, which is one comma one.

Using point-slope form, y minus one equals four times x minus one.

This example separates three objects that students often mix together: f of one is the

output one; f prime of one is the slope four; and the tangent line is a new linear equation

built from both.

Always compute the point and the slope before writing the tangent line.

When solving a derivative problem from first principles, use five checks.

First, identify the two inputs and the interval change h.

Second, form the difference quotient with parentheses around the entire numerator.

Third, simplify while h is nonzero.

Fourth, take the limit only after the removable algebraic obstacle is handled.

Fifth, interpret the result with a slope, rate, units, or tangent line.

Common errors include substituting zero too early, dropping parentheses, confusing f of a

with f prime of a, and assuming every continuous point is differentiable.

The algebra matters, but the interpretation is what tells you whether the answer belongs to

the original problem.

The derivative begins with two points and an average rate of change.

A limit brings those points together without dividing by zero.

When the limit exists, it gives the tangent slope and instantaneous rate at one input.

Repeating the process across inputs creates the derivative function.

Its sign and value describe local behavior, its failures mark important geometric

features, its physical units describe rates such as velocity, and its value supplies the

slope of a local linear approximation.

These are not unrelated definitions.

They are different views of the same mathematical object.

If you can move fluently among the quotient, the graph, the units, and the application, you

understand the derivative at a foundation strong enough for the rules and applications

that follow.
