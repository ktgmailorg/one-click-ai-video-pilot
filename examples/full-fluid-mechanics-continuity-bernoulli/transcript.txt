We begin by isolating a specific region of flowing fluid, which we call a control volume.

Imagine drawing a transparent box around a section of a water pipe.

This boundary is purely imaginary, but it allows us to track exactly what enters and

leaves without worrying about the fluid outside.

The control volume concept transforms a complex, continuous system into a manageable

mathematical framework.

By focusing only on the mass crossing this imaginary surface, we can apply conservation

laws directly to the flow.

This approach is essential because it separates the internal dynamics from external

influences, letting us analyze steady conditions systematically.

We will now examine how mass behaves within this defined space.

Density measures how much mass occupies a given volume, acting as the bridge between

mass and space.

In our control volume, density determines how tightly packed the fluid molecules are.

If we compress the fluid, density increases, meaning more mass fits into the same volume.

Conversely, heating the fluid expands it, lowering density.

This property is crucial because it dictates how the fluid responds to pressure changes and

area variations.

We treat density as a fundamental material property that remains constant for liquids but

can shift for gases.

Recognizing density allows us to predict how mass distributes itself within our imaginary

boundary before analyzing motion.

Mass flow rate quantifies how much mass crosses a plane each second.

Imagine a conveyor belt carrying boxes; the mass flow rate tells you exactly how many

boxes pass a checkpoint per minute.

Mathematically, we multiply density by the cross-sectional area and the average velocity

to get this value.

This formula works because density gives mass per volume, area gives volume per length, and

velocity gives length per time.

When you combine them, volume and length cancel out, leaving mass per time.

This calculation is foundational because it converts spatial dimensions into a temporal

rate, allowing us to track material movement through our control volume.

Continuity enforces mass conservation within our control volume.

If mass enters at a certain rate, it must exit at the exact same rate when the flow is

steady.

No mass accumulates inside the box, and no sources or sinks exist within it.

This principle means the product of density, area, and velocity remains constant along the

pipe.

If the pipe narrows, the fluid must speed up to maintain the same mass flow rate.

Conversely, if the pipe widens, the fluid slows down.

This relationship holds regardless of the fluid type, provided we account for density

changes in compressible gases.

Continuity is the first law of fluid mechanics in action, dictating how geometry forces

velocity to adjust.

Pressure in a flowing fluid splits into two distinct components.

Static pressure measures the fluid's inherent thermodynamic energy, independent of motion.

Dynamic pressure quantifies the kinetic energy associated with the fluid's velocity.

When fluid moves faster, dynamic pressure increases while static pressure typically

decreases.

This trade-off occurs because total energy remains constant in ideal conditions.

We visualize this by imagining a stationary observer measuring static pressure with a

wall-mounted gauge, versus a moving observer experiencing dynamic pressure as a ram effect.

Understanding both components allows us to predict how forces distribute across pipe

walls and how velocity changes affect pressure readings.

Bernoulli's equation links static pressure, dynamic pressure, and total pressure along a

streamline.

It states that the sum of static pressure and one-half density times velocity squared equals

a constant total pressure.

This relationship emerges from energy conservation, assuming steady, inviscid,

incompressible flow without heat addition, shaft work, or significant elevation changes.

These assumptions simplify reality but are critical for accurate predictions.

If viscosity, compressibility, pumps, turbines, heat transfer, or unsteady behavior

materially affect the flow, Bernoulli's simplified form fails.

We must verify these conditions before applying the equation to real-world systems.

Combining continuity and Bernoulli's equation reveals how pipe geometry dictates pressure

and velocity.

When a pipe narrows, continuity forces velocity to increase.

Bernoulli's equation then predicts that this velocity increase must be offset by a drop in

static pressure.

Conversely, a widening pipe reduces velocity and raises static pressure.

This inverse relationship between area and pressure is fundamental to designing ducts,

nozzles, and Venturi meters.

We can visualize this by tracking pressure gauges along a tapered pipe: readings will

fall in the narrow section and rise in the wide section.

These relationships allow engineers to predict flow behavior without complex simulations.

Pitot measurement exploits the area-velocity-pressure relationship to

determine fluid speed.

A Pitot tube points directly into the flow, capturing dynamic pressure at its stagnation

point.

A static port measures the ambient static pressure.

The difference between these two readings equals dynamic pressure, which we convert to

velocity using Bernoulli's equation.

This technique is widely used in aviation and HVAC systems to map airflow patterns.

By placing multiple Pitot probes along a duct, we can reconstruct the velocity profile and

verify theoretical predictions.

The method relies on accurate pressure sensing and assumes the flow remains undisturbed by

the probe itself.

Real flows rarely meet ideal assumptions, introducing energy losses that invalidate

simple Bernoulli calculations.

Friction against pipe walls, turbulence, and sudden expansions dissipate mechanical energy

into heat.

These losses reduce the total pressure available for flow, meaning static and dynamic

pressure sums will not remain constant.

We must recognize invalid applications when viscosity, compressibility, pumps, turbines,

heat transfer, or unsteady behavior materially affect the flow.

In such cases, we switch to the general energy equation or use empirical loss coefficients.

Identifying these limitations prevents erroneous predictions and ensures engineering

designs account for real-world inefficiencies.

Let us apply these concepts to a narrowing-pipe example.

Suppose water flows through a pipe with an inlet area of 0.

05 square meters at 2 meters per second.

The outlet area shrinks to 0.

025 square meters.

Using continuity, we find the outlet velocity doubles to 4 meters per second.

Assuming incompressible flow, density remains 1000 kilograms per cubic meter.

Applying Bernoulli's equation, we calculate the static pressure drop.

The dynamic pressure increases by half density times the velocity change squared, yielding a

measurable pressure difference.

This step-by-step calculation demonstrates how area reduction forces velocity increase and

static pressure decrease, completing our practical analysis.
